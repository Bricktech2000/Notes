> delimited continuations

> They differ in what the captured continuation does when you invoke it.
>
> For a context `E[·]` delimited by `reset` or `prompt`:
>
> - **`shift`/`reset`** captures the continuation up to the nearest `reset`, and when the captured continuation `k` is resumed, the delimiter is reinstated around the resumed computation:
>
>   `reset(E[shift f])  ↦  reset(f (λx. reset(E[x])))`
>
> - **`control`/`prompt`** captures the continuation up to the nearest `prompt`, but resuming `k` does **not** automatically reinstall the delimiter:
>
>   `prompt(E[control f])  ↦  prompt(f (λx. E[x]))`
>
> So the operational difference is:
>
> - `shift` is **re-delimited on resumption**.
> - `control` is **not re-delimited on resumption**.
>
> That one distinction has the usual consequences: `shift/reset` behaves more compositionally and is easier to nest predictably, while `control/prompt` is the more direct “raw” continuation-capture operator. Naming conventions vary a bit across papers, but this is the standard semantic distinction.

> I watched a talk by Alexis King and she defined `prompt(E[control f]) ↦ f (λx. E[x])`

> Yes. There are multiple closely related calculi in the literature, and different authors choose slightly different reduction rules.
>
> What you saw from Alexis King is the formulation usually associated with **Felleisen's `control`/`prompt`**:
>
> $$
> \text{prompt}(E[\text{control}\ f])
> ;\to;
> f(\lambda x.,E[x])
> $$
>
> with the crucial side condition that $E$ contains no enclosing `prompt`.
>
> Notice that the outer `prompt` disappears entirely. The captured continuation is just
>
> $$
> k = \lambda x.,E[x]
> $$
>
> with no reinstallation of a delimiter.
>
> By contrast, `shift/reset` is typically given as
>
> $$
> \text{reset}(E[\text{shift}\ f])
> ;\to;
> \text{reset}\bigl(f(\lambda x.,\text{reset}(E[x]))\bigr)
> $$
>
> where both:
>
> 1. the body of `f` remains inside a delimiter, and
> 2. invocations of the captured continuation reinstall a delimiter.
>
> Those are exactly the features that `control` lacks.
>
> A useful way to think about it is:
>
> | Operator pair    | What happens when continuation is resumed? |
> | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
> | `shift/reset`    | Resumption is automatically re-delimited.  |
> | `control/prompt` | Resumption is not re-delimited.            |
>
> The rule I gave previously,
>
> $$
> \text{prompt}(E[\text{control}\ f])
> \to
> \text{prompt}(f(\lambda x.E[x]))
> $$
>
> is a variant used in some presentations, but the classic `control/prompt` semantics from Felleisen's hierarchy is the one Alexis King was almost certainly discussing, where the surrounding `prompt` is removed by the reduction itself.
>
> So if you're comparing the standard forms:
>
> $$
> \text{prompt}(E[\text{control}\ f])
> \to
> f(\lambda x.E[x])
> $$
>
> versus
>
> $$
> \text{reset}(E[\text{shift}\ f])
> \to
> \text{reset}(f(\lambda x.\text{reset}(E[x])))
> $$
>
> the difference is even more striking: `shift` keeps and reinstalls delimiters; `control` captures the context and escapes the prompt entirely.
